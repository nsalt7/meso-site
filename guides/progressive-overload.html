Progressive overload that’s earned, not scheduled

Progressive overload means doing a little more over time so your muscles keep a reason to grow. The common mistake is putting the increase on a calendar: five more pounds every week, one more set every week, whether or not you could use them. The better rule is simple. Add when your logbook shows you beat last week.

Scheduled versus earned

A scheduled plan decides in advance. It works until your body disagrees, and then you are grinding a number that no longer fits. An earned plan decides from what you actually did. In trials, adjusting training from performance matched or beat fixed programs (Mann 2010; Helms 2018).

Weight: double progression

Work inside a rep window, say 8 to 12. Keep the weight and add reps until you hit the top of the window on every set at your target effort. Then add the smallest practical jump, about 2.5 to 5%, and start climbing reps again from near the bottom.

Rep progression and load progression grow muscle about equally when effort is matched (Plotkin 2022), so a rep gained is progress just as real as plates added. The jump size is convention; the principle, chase reps first and let them earn the weight, is the evidence.

If you miss the bottom of the window on a lift two weeks running, back the load off about 5% and rebuild. Re-anchoring to what you can do beats grinding a stale number.

Sets: earn them

More weekly sets grow more muscle, with returns that shrink as volume gets high (Schoenfeld 2017; Pelland 2025). So it is tempting to pile them on. The evidence says unearned volume buys little: trained lifters who jumped 30 to 60% above their usual sets for eight weeks grew no more than those who stayed put (Barsuhn 2025), and 18 or 24 weekly sets did no better than 12 in another trial (Aube 2022). Adding sets step by step did give a small edge in a 12-week trial (Enes 2024).

Put those together and the rule is clear: add a set when the muscle shows it is recovering, by beating last week on its lifts. Stall, and hold. Regress, and drop one. One set per step is convention; the direction is evidence.

What counts as beating last week

Compare your best set on the same exercise against last week's. One more rep at the same weight is a beat. More weight with reps still inside your window is a beat too, or at worst holds steady, because losing a rep or two right after a weight jump is the plan working. Use the same effort target both weeks, or the comparison means nothing.

Compare like with like: the same movement, not the same slot in your program. If you swap an exercise, its history starts fresh.

What does not earn more work

Soreness tracks muscle damage and novelty, not growth, and muscle grows fine without it (Damas 2016; Damas 2018). The pump feels great, but as a growth signal it has mechanistic support only (Schoenfeld & Contreras 2014). Use how you feel as a brake: if you are still sore at the next session or the workload felt like too much, hold or pull back. Never let feeling wrecked, or pumped, add sets.

When you cannot add weight

At home or on the bars, the lever is a harder variation or more reps instead of plates. Same rule: earn it. See calisthenics progressions.

How Meso runs it

Meso calls this Earned Volume. Each lift climbs its rep window, then the load jumps when you top it on every set. Each muscle earns one more weekly set when you beat last week, holds when you stall, and drops when you regress, up to a ceiling well below where extra sets stop paying. Soreness, workload and joint pain can only hold or cut. A sustained slide brings the deload forward.
