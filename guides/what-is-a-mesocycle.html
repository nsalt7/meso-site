Meso training guide

What is a mesocycle?

A mesocycle is a block of training, usually four to six weeks, with one job: build up the work week by week, then back off for a week so the fatigue clears and the progress shows. String blocks together and you have a training year.

The three cycles

Coaches describe training in three nested units:

  • Microcycle: one repeating unit, usually a week. Your Monday to Sunday plan.
  • Mesocycle: a block of several microcycles with a direction. In hypertrophy training that direction is usually more effort and more sets as the weeks go on.
  • Macrocycle: the long view, a season or a year of mesocycles.

The labels come from periodization, the coaching tradition of planning training in phases. They are a way of organising the work, not a physiological law.

What happens inside a hypertrophy block

A typical block has accumulation weeks and a deload week:

  • Effort climbs. Sets start a few reps short of failure and finish closer to it. Growth improves as sets approach failure (Robinson 2024), so the hardest weeks come late, when you are calibrated to the lifts. See reps in reserve, explained.
  • Load and reps climb. You beat last week on the same movements: one more rep, or a little more weight once the reps are there. See progressive overload that is earned.
  • Volume can climb. More weekly hard sets grow more muscle, with diminishing returns as the number gets high (Schoenfeld 2017; Pelland 2025). A block is where those extra sets get added, a step at a time.
  • It ends in a deload. A lighter week that keeps the movements and cuts the effort. See deload weeks: when and how.

Why train in blocks at all

Fatigue builds across hard weeks and can hide the strength you have gained. Running the same hard week forever tends to end in stalled lifts and achy joints. A block gives you a planned ramp and a planned exit, and it gives you a clean comparison: the same exercises, week after week, so you can tell whether you are actually getting stronger in the rep ranges that grow muscle.

That comparison is the real value. If you swap exercises every session you never find out what is working.

How long should a mesocycle be?

Four to six weeks is the common range, and it is convention, not a number a trial has pinned down. Shorter blocks spend a bigger share of your time deloading; longer ones risk the fatigue outrunning the progress. Pick a length you will finish. If you are newer, the shorter end is fine, because you will still be calibrating your loads in week one.

What the research does support is the content of the block: weekly volume drives growth with diminishing returns (Pelland 2025), effort matters more as you get near failure (Robinson 2024), and a deload that reduces training stress rather than stopping it is the expert consensus (Bell 2023).

Between blocks

Finish a block strong and start the next one a touch below the volume you peaked at, then earn the climb again. Keep the lifts that moved well; swap anything that argued with your joints. Adding sets you have not earned buys little: trained lifters who jumped 30 to 60% above their usual weekly sets for eight weeks grew no more than those who stayed put (Barsuhn 2025).

How Meso runs a mesocycle

In Meso you choose a block of 4, 5 or 6 weeks; the last week is always the deload. Compounds ramp from 3 reps in reserve to 1 across the block. Each muscle earns one more weekly set only when you beat last week on its lifts, holds when you stall, and drops when you regress. If performance falls two weeks running across several muscles, the deload comes early. When the block ends, the next one starts one set below where you finished, so volume builds block to block until each muscle reaches its ceiling.

Let the logbook decideMeso runs these rules for you: log your sets and it writes next week from what you actually lifted. Works offline, no account.
